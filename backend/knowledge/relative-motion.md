# Relative Motion at Closest Approach

Relative velocity at TCA is the magnitude of the velocity difference between
the two objects at the moment of closest approach. It describes how fast
the objects sweep past each other.

## Typical magnitudes

In low Earth orbit, orbital speeds are around 7-8 km/s. Two objects in
similar prograde orbits may pass at only a few hundred meters per second
relative speed, but most conjunctions involve crossing geometries where
relative velocities reach 7-15 km/s. At 10 km/s, an object travels 10
kilometers every second — several times a typical miss distance each
second.

## Crossing geometries

- Co-orbital or near-coplanar encounters: similar inclinations and altitudes
  produce slow relative motion; small position errors translate into long
  uncertainty windows in time.
- Head-on and crossing orbits: counter-rotating or steeply inclined orbits
  produce very high relative velocities; the encounter is over in fractions
  of a second.

## Why high relative velocity raises concern

High relative velocity matters even when the miss distance looks moderate:

1. Timing sensitivity: at 12 km/s, being wrong about the TCA by just one
   second shifts the encounter geometry by 12 km — larger than many
   screening thresholds.
2. Collision consequence: impact energy scales with the square of relative
   velocity; hypervelocity impacts above ~1 km/s vaporize material and
   create clouds of fragments regardless of object size.
3. Less reaction margin: fast geometry leaves little room between "pass
   safely" and "impact" if predictions are off.

Consequently, high-speed crossings with sub-25-km misses are treated as
genuinely risky rather than benign fly-bys.
