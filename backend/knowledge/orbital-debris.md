# The Orbital Debris Environment

Orbital debris (space junk) consists of defunct satellites, spent rocket
stages, mission-related objects, and fragments from breakups. Only a
fraction of tracked objects are operational spacecraft.

## Scale of the problem

The US Space Surveillance Network tracks over 45,000 objects larger than
roughly 10 cm in its public catalog, of which well over half belong to the
debris population. Statistical models (such as ESA's MASTER model) estimate
over one million debris fragments between 1 cm and 10 cm — too small to
track reliably yet energetic enough to destroy an operating satellite on
impact.

## Major events

Two events shaped today's environment:

- Fengyun-1C ASAT test (January 2007): China destroyed its own weather
  satellite at ~865 km altitude with a missile, creating over 3,400
  cataloged fragments — the single worst debris-generating event ever.
- Iridium 33 / Cosmos 2251 collision (February 2009): the first accidental
  hypervelocity collision between two intact satellites, at ~790 km over
  Siberia, adding more than 2,000 tracked fragments.

Together these two events account for a large share of all cataloged debris.

## LEO density

Debris is concentrated in low Earth orbit, especially in dense bands near
800-900 km and at sun-synchronous inclinations (~98 degrees), exactly where
many Earth observation and reconnaissance satellites operate. Collision
risk is not uniform: operators in these bands screen every object pair
continuously and maneuver several times per year.
